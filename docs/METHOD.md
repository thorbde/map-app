# Method

This project is a long-term memory experiment. I write one short essay about each country in the world, and I want to still remember what I wrote years from now. The writing process and review routine below are built on well-replicated findings from cognitive psychology about how people learn and remember.

## The principles

| Principle | What the research shows | How it's used here |
|---|---|---|
| **Retrieval practice** | Recalling information from memory strengthens long-term retention far more than re-reading it, and more than many "deeper" study techniques (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006; Karpicke & Blunt, 2011). | Every essay is drafted with sources closed. Every review is a recall attempt before looking. |
| **Spaced practice** | Reviewing at expanding intervals beats massed study. The best gap grows with how long you want to remember (Cepeda et al., 2006, 2008). | A fixed review schedule after each essay: 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, then roughly every 6 months. |
| **Pretesting** | Trying to answer before studying, even unsuccessfully, improves later learning of that material (Richland, Kornell & Kao, 2009). | Each essay starts with a five-minute "what do I already know?" attempt. |
| **Feedback and error correction** | Retrieval works best followed by feedback. Errors made with high confidence are corrected especially well once caught (Butterfield & Metcalfe, 2001). | After the closed-book draft, I check everything against sources and log what I got wrong. |
| **Elaborative interrogation** | Asking and answering "why?" questions improves memory compared with reading facts alone (Pressley et al., 1987; Dunlosky et al., 2013). | Each essay explains at least two "why"s, not just "what"s. |
| **Self-explanation and prior knowledge** | Explaining how new information connects to what you already know deepens understanding (Chi et al., 1994). | Each essay links the country to countries I've already written about and to things I know. |
| **Generation effect** | Information you produce yourself is remembered better than information you only read (Slamecka & Graf, 1978). | Everything is written in my own words, including the memory hook and my own recall questions. |
| **Dual coding** | Combining words with visuals gives memory two routes to the same information (Paivio, 1986). | Each essay includes a hand-drawn map sketched from memory, with its cities, and the site places every city on a map. |
| **Interleaving** | Mixing different items during review improves the ability to tell them apart, compared with reviewing them in blocks (Kornell & Bjork, 2008). | Reviews and quizzes draw countries in random order across regions, not in the order they were written. |
| **Desirable difficulties** | Conditions that make learning feel harder in the moment (recall instead of re-reading, spacing, mixing) often produce better long-term retention (Bjork, 1994). | The umbrella principle. If a step feels effortful, that is usually the point. |

**What I deliberately avoid:** re-reading as review, highlighting, and copying from sources. The large review by Dunlosky et al. (2013) rates these as low-utility. Summarising on its own also rates low, which is why the essays are not summaries written with the sources open. The value comes from the retrieval, explanation and feedback built into the process.

## Writing an essay (about 2 hours)

**1. Pretest (5–10 min, closed book).** Before reading anything, write down everything I know or think I know about the country, even if it's almost nothing. Then write three questions I'm curious about.

**2. Study (30 min).** Read at least two sources. Take sparse notes in my own words, look for answers to my three questions, and keep asking *why*.

**3. Closed-book draft (30–45 min).** Close the sources and the notes. Write the essay from memory. Where I can't remember, write `[CHECK]` and move on.

**4. Feedback (15–20 min).** Open the sources again and fill in the `[CHECK]` markers. Correct anything wrong and note it under *What I got wrong*, especially things I was sure about.

**5. Encode for review (15 min).** Write the memory hook, and sketch the country's map from memory: outline, neighbours, the cities I wrote about, and one or two key features. Write five recall questions with answers, mixing fact questions with "why" questions, with at least one about a city. Also write one **progressive-clue riddle**: three to five clues that point to one of the cities or to the country, from obscure to obvious. Writing it means recalling in the reverse direction, from detail to place, and it doubles as a quiz for later.

## Essay structure (400–600 words, plus the extras)

- **Facts:** capital, languages, population, independence, neighbours, government. These come from Wikidata and are kept up to date by the site, so I don't copy them in. Copying isn't learning. I learn them by retrieval instead (see *Reviewing*).
- **Geography and nature:** where it is, what the land is like, how that shapes life there
- **Cities:** the capital plus the two to four largest or most important cities. For each one: where it lies (region, river, coast), why it grew there, what it's known for, and one concrete detail such as a landmark, an event, a person or a nickname
- **History:** two or three turning points, with the reasons behind them
- **The country today:** economy, politics, the big current question
- **Culture:** one or two things, in depth
- **A person:** someone historical or contemporary, and why they matter
- **Why?** At least two "why" questions answered in my own words
- **Connections:** links to countries I've already written about and to things I know
- **Three things I didn't know**
- **What I got wrong** in the closed-book draft
- **Before I started:** the pretest, kept for the record
- **Sources**
- **Recall questions** (five, with answers) and **one progressive-clue riddle**, both stored in the frontmatter

## Reviewing

- **Free recall first.** Take a blank page and write everything I remember about the country, *then* check. The site's "Test me" mode supports this.
- **Facts by testing, not re-reading.** The facts sit next to every essay, but I learn them by hiding them and recalling: the site's memorization mode turns them into flashcards and quizzes.
- **Spaced schedule:** review at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month and 3 months, then about every 6 months. Anki, or the site's review list, keeps track of when each country is due.
- **Mixed order.** Review countries randomly across regions, and use the map quiz and the riddles. In the riddles, the fewer clues I need, the better.
- **Built-in cumulative retrieval.** The *Connections* section in every new essay forces recall of earlier countries, so writing new essays also reviews old ones.
- **Region synthesis.** After finishing a region, write one page from memory about what its countries share and what sets them apart.
- **Translation as review.** Later, essays are translated into other languages *from memory*, without the original open, and then compared with the original. That is retrieval and feedback in one.

## A note on evidence

These principles are among the most robust findings in learning research, but most studies use students and lab materials over weeks or months. This project applies them to a personal, multi-year format, so it is an application of the research and not a test of it.
